# How to Compare Job Offers: A Practical Checklist Beyond the Salary Number

Getting one offer is a relief. Getting two at the same time, or an offer while you are still deep in another company’s process, is a different kind of stress. The numbers look comparable, the recruiters want an answer by Friday, and every gut feeling contradicts the last one.

This guide gives you a structured way to compare offers so that you decide on facts, not on whoever called last. It works for one offer versus your current job, too.

## Step 1: Get every offer in writing first

Do not compare a verbal promise with a written document. Before you start weighing anything, ask each company for the offer in writing, even if it is just a detailed email. It should cover at least:

- base salary and currency, gross or net, and how often it is paid
- contract type (employment, contractor, B2B or private entrepreneur arrangement)
- bonus structure and whether it is guaranteed or discretionary
- equity or options, if any, with the vesting schedule
- paid time off, sick leave and public holidays
- start date, probation period and notice period
- remote, hybrid or office policy, written explicitly
- job title and the team or manager you will report to

If something you were told in an interview is missing from the written offer, ask about it politely. A friendly line is enough: “Thanks for the offer! Could you confirm the remote policy and the probation length in writing?”

## Step 2: Convert everything to the same unit

Offers rarely arrive in the same shape. One is a monthly gross figure in local currency, another is an annual figure in USD for a contractor, a third adds a yearly bonus. Before you compare, bring them to one unit, for example net monthly income in the currency you actually spend.

Things to normalize:

1. **Gross vs net.** Work out what you take home after taxes and mandatory contributions for that contract type in your country. If you are unsure, ask an accountant or check official tax guidance rather than guessing.
2. **Contractor costs.** As a contractor you usually pay your own taxes, accounting, unpaid vacation days and sometimes equipment. A contractor rate that looks higher can end up close to an employment salary once you subtract these.
3. **Paid vs unpaid time off.** If one offer pays for 20 vacation days and another pays for none, the second one effectively pays you for fewer days per year.
4. **Bonuses.** Count only the guaranteed part as income. Treat discretionary bonuses as a nice possibility, not as salary.
5. **Equity.** Unless it is in a publicly traded company, treat it as a lottery ticket with an unknown value. It can be a tiebreaker, rarely the reason to accept.

A simple rule of thumb: if you cannot explain in one sentence where a number comes from, it does not belong in your comparison yet.

## Step 3: Score what money cannot show

Two offers with the same net income can lead to very different years. Write down the factors that matter to you and score each offer on them. Here is a table you can copy:

| Factor | Weight (1-3) | Offer A (1-5) | Offer B (1-5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net monthly income | 3 | | |
| Growth: what you will learn, title, scope | 3 | | |
| Manager and team impression | 3 | | |
| Product and tech stack | 2 | | |
| Remote policy and commute | 2 | | |
| Stability of the company | 2 | | |
| Workload and on-call expectations | 2 | | |
| Benefits: health insurance, learning budget, equipment | 1 | | |

Multiply each score by its weight and add them up. The total will not make the decision for you, but it often reveals that the offer you were leaning toward wins on just one big factor, or that the “boring” option is ahead on almost everything.

Choose the weights before you fill in the scores. If you set them afterwards, you will unconsciously tune them to justify the choice you already wanted.

## Step 4: Ask the questions you skipped in the interviews

During interviews you were selling yourself. Now the company is selling to you, which makes this the best moment to ask direct questions. Good ones:

- What would a great first three months look like in this role?
- Why is this position open: growth or someone leaving?
- How are performance reviews and salary revisions done, and how often?
- What does a normal week look like for the team in terms of meetings and on-call?
- Can I talk to someone who would be my peer on the team?

How the company answers matters as much as what it says. Vague answers to simple questions about reviews or workload are information too.

## Step 5: Handle the timing without burning bridges

The most common problem is not choosing; it is that offers arrive at different times. Some practical moves:

- **Ask for time.** Saying “I’m very interested and want to make a careful decision. Could I get back to you by next Wednesday?” is normal. A few business days is a reasonable request in most markets.
- **Tell the slower company.** If you have an offer and prefer another company that is still interviewing, let their recruiter know you have a deadline. Many companies can speed up final steps when they know.
- **Do not invent offers.** Mentioning a competing offer is fine when it is real. Bluffing tends to backfire, especially in small industries where recruiters talk.
- **Use the other offer carefully in negotiation.** If you prefer Company B but Company A pays more, you can tell B honestly: “You are my first choice. I have another offer at a higher level. Is there flexibility on the base salary?” Keep it factual and calm.

## Step 6: Check the red flags

Some signals deserve extra weight, whatever the salary:

- pressure to accept within hours, with no room for questions
- the written offer differs from what was agreed verbally and nobody explains why
- refusal to put the remote policy, probation or contract type in writing
- unclear who your manager will be
- reviews from former employees describing the same problem again and again, especially if the interviewers avoided the topic

One red flag is not always a dealbreaker. Two or three together usually are.

## Step 7: Decide, then close every loop

Once you have decided, act quickly and politely on both sides:

1. Accept the chosen offer in writing and confirm the start date.
2. Decline the other offers the same day if possible. A short, warm message keeps the door open: “Thank you for the offer and for the time your team spent with me. After careful thought I have accepted another position. I hope our paths cross again.”
3. Withdraw from any processes that are still running, so recruiters do not keep scheduling interviews for you.
4. Do not resign from your current job until you have the signed offer or contract in hand.

## Keep the comparison in one place

When offers overlap, details get lost fast: who promised what, which deadline belongs to which company, what you asked and what they answered. ## Quick checklist

- Every offer in writing, with contract type, currency and remote policy
- All money converted to net monthly income in one currency
- Weights chosen before scores
- Questions asked about reviews, workload and why the role is open
- Deadlines agreed, slower companies informed
- Red flags noted honestly
- Accepted in writing, others declined politely, current job left only after signing

A good decision is not one that turns out perfect. It is one you made with all the facts on the table, and that you can explain to yourself a year from now.
